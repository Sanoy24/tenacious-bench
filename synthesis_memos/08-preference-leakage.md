# Synthesis Memo 08: Preference Leakage

## Reading

David Li et al., *Preference Leakage: A Contamination Problem in LLM-as-a-Judge*, 2025. `arXiv:2502.01534`

## Thesis

When the same model (or a closely related model) both generates candidate outputs and judges them, the judge systematically favors outputs from its own family regardless of actual quality. This "preference leakage" operates through three relatedness relationships: same model, parent-child inheritance, and same family (e.g., GPT-3.5 variants). The bias is strongest for subjective evaluation tasks (writing, programming) and weakest for objective ones (math). Severity correlates with the proportion of synthetic training data the judge was trained on — models with more self-generated training data leak more strongly. The contamination is hard to detect because it is not a benchmark overlap problem; it is a distributional preference pattern baked into the judge's weights.

## Useful idea for this project

The core takeaway applies directly to Path B: never use the same model to generate the "chosen" rewrites in training pairs AND serve as the judge being trained. For Tenacious-Bench, the judge backbone is Qwen 3.5 (0.8B or 2B). The "chosen" outputs — the corrected outreach drafts that serve as the preferred side of each preference pair — must not come from Qwen 3.5. If they do, the judge will learn to prefer Qwen-style phrasings rather than evidence-calibrated content, and it will do so invisibly (both outputs are fluent English, the bias shows up only in aggregate statistics).

The practical prevention rule the paper derives is clear: rotate model families between generation and judgment. This is feasible here at low cost because OpenRouter's dev-tier models include both Qwen-family and non-Qwen-family options.

## My disagreement

The paper recommends maximally distant model families as the primary safeguard against preference leakage. My disagreement is that family distance is a defense-in-depth measure, not a primary control — and treating it as primary can create a false sense of safety while missing the actual vulnerability.

Here is the concrete problem: the paper's finding applies to cases where the ground truth for "chosen" vs. "rejected" is subjective or soft. In those cases, the generator's family affiliation is the largest confound because there is no objective standard to override the judge's preference patterns. For Tenacious-Bench, the ground truth is not soft. `scoring_evaluator.py` runs deterministic checks — banned phrase lists, regex stack-commitment detection, required-phrase gating, word count limits. A "chosen" output that fails any critical check is rejected from the training data entirely, regardless of which model wrote it. This is an objective, family-independent quality filter that the Preference Leakage paper's setup does not have.

Family distance matters most when the evaluation signal is entirely learned (a model judge making holistic quality judgments). When the evaluation signal is externally grounded in deterministic rules, family distance becomes secondary. The primary control is evaluator-pass validation; family rotation is the secondary control.

The paper's experimental setup also tests on benchmarks where the "wrong" answer is defined by GPT-4 agreement, not by an independent oracle. That circularity inflates the measured leakage because GPT-4's family preferences define both the correct answer and the contamination pattern. Tenacious-Bench uses a hand-authored, probe-grounded rubric as the oracle — this is structurally different.

## Implementation consequence

Two-layer leakage prevention:

The primary control is evaluator-pass validation. Every "chosen" output in `training_data/` must pass `scoring_evaluator.py` with `passed_all_checks: true`; every "rejected" output must fail with `passed_all_checks: false`. No pair enters training unless both conditions hold. This check is family-independent and not gameable by style preferences.

The secondary control is family rotation. "Chosen" rewrites are generated by DeepSeek V3.2 (a non-Qwen-family model) via OpenRouter dev-tier. The judge backbone is Qwen 3.5. This rotation is documented in `methodology_rationale.md` with the specific model IDs and is verifiable from `generation_scripts/synthesis_cost_log.json`.

The paper's most important practical warning — "preference leakage is harder to detect than other biases" — does apply here, and it justifies keeping the family rotation as a secondary measure even though the primary evaluator guard already mitigates the core risk. Absence of evidence of leakage is not evidence of absence; the rotation costs nothing and prevents a class of failure that would not show up until the ablation table.
